[Dossier: Aral Sea Crisis & IFAS | Sec 1: Pre-Crisis Baseline]
In the recent past, the Aral Sea was a unique saline lake with a volume of 1,083 km3 and a surface area of 68,000 km2, supporting high biological productivity and a distinct aquatic ecosystem before large-scale river diversions began.
---
[Dossier: Aral Sea Crisis & IFAS | Sec 2: 20th-Century Shrinkage]
During the second half of the 20th century, massive land-reclamation programs in the Aral Sea basin doubled irrigated acreage and water withdrawals. By 2000, the sea's water level had fallen by more than 20 meters, its volume shrank 6.4-fold, and its surface area dwindled more than threefold.
---
[Dossier: Aral Sea Crisis & IFAS | Sec 3: Socio-Economic & Health Impact]
The Aral Sea ecological disaster degraded living conditions for over 50 million residents across Central Asia, driving falling incomes, cultural loss, rising out-migration, lower life expectancy, higher infant mortality, and increased bacterial and viral diseases.
---
[Dossier: Aral Sea Crisis & IFAS | Sec 4A: Creation & Mission of IFAS]
In 1993, the Heads of Central Asian States established the International Fund for Saving the Aral Sea (IFAS) to address the sea's desiccation, prevent wider socio-economic and ecological collapse across the basin, and coordinate joint action with international partners.
---
[Dossier: Aral Sea Crisis & IFAS | Sec 4B: 2026 IFAS & Basin Updates]
Within IFAS—following the April 2026 Astana Summit—member states pursue Aral Sea Basin Programs, North Aral Sea restoration via the Kokaral Dam (targeting 27 to 34 km3 volume), saxaul afforestation on the dry Aralkum seabed, and Uzbekistan's $3.3 billion package of 133 projects for its 2027–2029 IFAS chairmanship.
