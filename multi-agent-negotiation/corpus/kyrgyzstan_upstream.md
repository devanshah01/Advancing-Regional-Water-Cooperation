Kyrgyzstan is a high-altitude, upper mountain source contributor. It controls headwater tributaries that feed the Amu Darya system (via the Pamir-Alai range) while most of its territory and irrigated agriculture sit in the separate Syr Darya basin. Glaciers in the Pamir-Alai and Tien Shan ranges act as natural water towers for the wider Central Asian region, and their recession under climate change threatens long-term baseline flows into both basins.

Kyrgyzstan was assigned 0.4 bcm (0.6 percent) of the 61.5 bcm Protocol 566 allocation signed by Soviet water authorities on 10 September 1987, the smallest share of any party to the agreement. After independence, Kyrgyzstan kept this share through the 1992 Almaty Agreement and the Interstate Commission for Water Coordination (ICWC), alongside Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan and Tajikistan.

Kyrgyzstan's domestic energy policy centers on hydroelectric capacity and national energy independence, aligning it with Tajikistan on the water-for-energy tradeoff: both upstream states prefer to store water during the growing season and release it in winter for electricity, a seasonal pattern that reduces downstream summer irrigation supply unless compensated. Kyrgyzstan relies on aging Soviet-era hydroelectric infrastructure with limited domestic capital for upgrades, environmental monitoring or disaster risk reduction (mudflows, landslides, glacial lake outbursts).

Kyrgyzstan and Kazakhstan experienced disputes over water in 2023, illustrating that upstream-downstream friction in the wider Aral Sea basin is not limited to the Amu Darya proper.

Kyrgyzstan's stated negotiating red lines are: no restriction on constructing upstream regulatory reservoirs, seasonal water storage facilities or micro-hydropower projects; and no veto by downstream states over its national energy and water policy exercised through historical Soviet-era allocation quotas, which it regards as a settlement it was never meaningfully party to.
